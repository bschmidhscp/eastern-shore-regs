CHAPTER 1 INTRODUCTION 
 
1.5 
CAROLINE COUNTY SEWER SERVICE NEEDS 
 
1.5.1 North County Region 
 
Background 
 
In 1996, the Town of Goldsboro, located in the northern region of the County, 
entered into a Consent Order with Maryland Department of the Environment 
(MDE) to construct a public sewer system to resolve the problem of failing septic 
systems in the Town.   Due to a lack of funding and other constraints, the Town 
was unable to comply with the terms of the Order, which required the Town to 
connect the individual septic systems operating within Town limits to a public 
sewerage system (see Chapter 5, Sewerage, Section 5.7.2. Election District 1 
(Henderson), ‘Maryland Department of Environment vs. Town of Goldsboro 
Consent Order’).  In 2006, the four towns in the north County region (Goldsboro, 
Henderson, Marydel and Templeville) and Caroline County became the subjects 
of a draft Consent Order (Proposed Order) prepared by MDE that sought to 
resolve the problem of failing septic systems on a regional scale by mandating 
the construction of a regional wastewater treatment facility to serve the four 
towns and rural properties adjacent to the towns.   
 
The Division of Environmental Health of the Caroline County Health Department 
confirms that many of the septic systems in northern Caroline County lack the 
necessary soil conditions to properly treat septic tank effluent.  Compounding 
the problem are the surrounding drainage ditches that penetrate the shallow 
groundwater and channel effluent from failing systems to streams and creeks 
that feed into the Choptank River. An additional threat is posed by the 
predominance of shallow wells in the region which may act as conduits for 
surface and groundwater contaminants to move into shallow and possibly 
deeper aquifers. None of these concerns have diminished over time.  A more 
alarming trend now is the installation of sewage holding tanks to abate failing 
systems.  The cost of providing ongoing maintenance to these tanks is often 
overwhelming for residents, resulting in tanks overflowing or backing up inside 
homes – an even greater public health threat.

On behalf of the Authority, Caroline County and the Maryland Environmental 
Service commissioned an Engineering Study, completed in October 2009, to 
determine the most appropriate type of regional wastewater system to serve the 
Authority's service area. Several engineering studies performed in the past 
provided recommendations for implementing wastewater systems, however, 
none of the recommendations were implemented, primarily due to the 
unavailability of funds. 
 
In the October 2009 Engineering Study, alternatives were evaluated for a 
regional wastewater collection, conveyance, treatment, and disposal system 
that would serve a minimum of 746 equivalent dwelling units (EDU) and a 
maximum of 1,532 EDUs. In the study, the use of a centralized system and de-
centralized, or multiple, systems for collection and treatment were evaluated.  A 
total of six alternative systems, or options, as well as a “no action” alternative 
were evaluated and compared.  A preliminary Environmental Assessment was 
performed for the recommended alternative. 
 
Based on a comparative analysis that addressed economic and non-economic 
factors, the study recommended the most cost-feasible alternative:  a low 
pressure (grinder pump) sewer collection system in each of the four towns and

In conjunction with COMAR regulations (26.03.01.04), the County has established 
a classification system for properties which will be served by water and sewer 
service. The system reflects a progression towards the extension or establishment 
of community water and sewer service to achieve County growth management 
goals as set forth in the County Water and Sewerage Plan and County 
Comprehensive Plan. 
 
The classification system is designed to show need and intent of the County, its 
municipalities and private developers for establishing or extending community 
water and sewer systems. The classification system is not intended to prevent 
development of community water and/or sewerage facilities prior to or later 
than the time periods indicated; rather it is the best estimate at the time of 
adoption as to when such development may be expected to occur. Every effort 
should be made by the County, municipalities, and developers to progress with 
water and sewer projects to the point that construction may occur in 
accordance with the Plan. However, there is no guarantee that having 
achieved one level water of sewer service timing, the next level will be granted.

may occur within 20 years may be assigned one of the following service timing 
classifications:   
W-1/S-1: areas served by community and multi-use water and sewerage systems

systems.

water supply and sewerage systems which are in the final planning stages. The

CHAPTER 5 SEWERAGE 
 
5.7.2. Election District No.1 (Henderson) 
 
Election District No. 1 (2010 population – 3,348), the northernmost election district, 
is comprised of the towns of Goldsboro, Henderson, Marydel and Templeville, 
and approximately 35 square miles of the unincorporated area of the County. 
With the exception of the mobile homes located in Caroline Acres and Cedars 
mobile home parks (located outside of the towns of Henderson and Marydel, 
respectively), the area is served by on-site sewage disposal (septic) systems.   
 
 
Water quality problems stemming from failing septic systems have been a 
regional public health issue since the 1970s.  Major concerns include: 
 
 
Goldsboro:  The Town’s 216 residents are served by private, individual water 
supply wells and septic systems.  Incidences of failing septic systems in the 
Town were recorded as early as 1975.   In 1996 the Maryland Department of 
the Environment (MDE) issued a Consent Order to the Town to resolve the 
problem of failing septic systems.   
 
 
Henderson: The Town’s 118 residents are served by a municipal water system 
and private, individual septic systems, a large number of which are failing.  
The Town’s public water system and elevated water storage tank water 
provide protection from septic contamination and the Town has thus far 
avoided serious public health issues as a result of contaminated drinking 
water.   
 
 
Marydel: the Town lies on both sides of the state line between Maryland and 
Delaware. The Town’s 147 Maryland residents are served by private individual 
wells and septic systems, most of which are suspected to be failing.  
Municipal and County officials are aware of public health issues stemming 
from contaminated groundwater in wells located within 100 feet of failing 
septic systems.

CHAPTER 1 INTRODUCTION 
 
1.5 
CAROLINE COUNTY SEWER SERVICE NEEDS 
 
1.5.1 North County Region 
 
Background 
 
In 1996, the Town of Goldsboro, located in the northern region of the County, entered 
into a Consent Order with Maryland Department of the Environment (MDE) to construct 
a public sewer system to resolve the problem of failing septic systems in the Town.   Due 
to a lack of funding and other constraints, the Town was unable to comply with the terms 
of the Order, which required the Town to connect the individual septic systems operating 
within Town limits to a public sewerage system (see Chapter 5, Sewerage, Section 
5.7.2. Election District 1 (Henderson), ‘Maryland Department of Environment vs. Town of 
Goldsboro Consent Order’).  In 2006, the four towns in the north County region 
(Goldsboro, Henderson, Marydel and Templeville) and Caroline County became the 
subjects of a draft Consent Order (Proposed Order) prepared by MDE that sought to 
resolve the problem of failing septic systems on a regional scale by mandating the 
construction of a regional wastewater treatment facility to serve the four towns and rural 
properties adjacent to the towns being served by failing or non-conforming septic 
systems.   
 
The Division of Environmental Health of the Caroline County Health Department 
confirms that many of the septic systems in northern Caroline County lack the necessary 
soil conditions to properly treat septic tank effluent.  Compounding the problem are the 
surrounding drainage ditches that penetrate the shallow groundwater and channel 
effluent from failing systems to streams and creeks that feed into the Choptank River. An 
additional threat is posed by the predominance of shallow wells in the region which may 
act as conduits for surface and groundwater contaminants to move into shallow and 
possibly deeper aquifers. None of these concerns have diminished over time.  A more 
alarming trend is the installation of sewage holding tanks to abate failing systems.  The 
cost of providing ongoing maintenance to these tanks is often overwhelming for 
residents, resulting in tanks overflowing or backing up inside homes – an even greater 
public health threat.

Marydel and Templeville formed the North County Water and Sewer Authority pursuant

3.4.8. Election District No.7 (Ridgely) 
 
The Town Ridgely water system currently serves a population of 1,868 via 747connections with 
an average daily demand of 167,000 gallons. The Town currently operates two active 
production wells, #2 and #3, drawing from the Piney Point Aquifer at a capacity of 108,000 and 
144,000 gallons per day respectively. The water quality in the aquifer is generally good. Each 
well has a depth of 310-350 feet, with 6” diameter. The wells operate 6-12 hours per day, 
depending on the time of year. The water is treated with chlorine prior to entering the tower. 
  
A Water Infrastructure Study was completed for the Town in 2007 and resulted in the 
recommendation of an additional elevated water storage tank with at least 500,000 gallons 
holding capacity. The system is permitted to withdraw an annual daily average of 200,000 gpd 
and a maximum of 300,000 gpd.  In 2006 and 2007, usage averaged about 140,000 gpd, 70 
percent of the system’s permitted capacity. The system’s current daily flow average of 106,000 
gpd coupled with the committed water allocation of 54,000 gpd represent an 80% withdrawal

<!-- page 204 -->
of the water tower’s capacity of 200,000 gallons per day. An additional 120,000 gpd is needed 
for the Town’s fire reserve flow needs, which indicates a potential water tower storage deficit.  
Sufficient water storage volume must be provided to allow for fire protection, emergency shut 
down reserve and equalization by well pumps (required storage tank volumes are calculated by 
combining these three elements of water demand).  A 2021 assessment of the existing water 
tower, built in 1984, indicated it is in urgent need of maintenance and rehabilitation.  The Town 
has been unable to complete this maintenance because taking the tower offline would impair 
potable water supply to the citizens; the Town does not have an adequate back-up or reserved 
capacity storage option to be activated when necessary maintenance is required on the 
existing tower. 
 
In 2023, to resolve this and the above-mentioned capacity issues, the Town plans to add an 
additional 300,000-gallon water tower to create the needed redundancy for maintenance and 
safety improvements, and allow for fire protection, emergency shut down reserve and 
equalization by well pumps. 
 
An additional well and new treatment facility are also planned as part of the Town’s water 
system improvements. The addition of one new well will serve the planned new water tower, 
serve as a back-up for the current wells, and meet the overtaxed current system demand. The 
system’s GAP permit will be amended to accommodate the additional new well and higher 
withdrawal capacity. A new water main will be constructed to connect the new water system 
components into the Town’s existing water system.  Further studies and water modeling will 
determine the best construction path and connection to the existing town system.
