**PUBLIC HEARING – ZONING TEXT AMENDMENT – An Act to amend Chapter 225 of the Wicomico County Code, titled “Zoning,” to establish and revise regulations pertaining to the installation, construction, location, and size of solar energy systems as necessary to comply with the requirements of Acts of Maryland 2025, Chapter 623 (Maryland Senate Bill 931), Annotated Code of Maryland. (K. Justice)** 

**The Public Hearing began at 4:15 p.m.**

Mr. Andrew Illuminati read the Public Hearing notice. 

Ms. Kaylee Justice provided a brief overview of the Planning Director’s Report for the Zoning Text Amendment, Chapter 623 of the Zoning Code, regarding solar energy systems and Maryland Senate Bill 931 (SB 931). Effective July 1, 2025, SB 931, otherwise known as the Renewable Energy Certainty Act, regulates solar energy development and preempts local zoning requirements. The proposed County Text Amendment updates zoning regulations in response to state law changes related to solar projects and limited local zoning authority over those projects. 

Planning staff recommends advancing a favorable recommendation to forward the proposed Zoning Text Amendments as presented with the following three (3) conditions: 

- 1) The proposed legislation is required per State law (SB 931) passed in Maryland’s 2025 Legislative Session. 

- 2) The State law (SB 931) is preemptive legislation which became effective July 1, 2025; and 3) Repealing the County’s outdated solar provisions will provide greater clarity and ensure the County Code does not contain regulations inconsistent with the State's current statutory framework. 

Mr. Illuminati reviewed the legislative bill and cited recommendations for solar energy systems. Recommendations included landscaping, permitted use, and decommissioning. Mr. Illuminati and Staff also recommended enumerating terms and definitions listed in the Text Amendment, noting items struck and modified to comply with State law. 

The County’s updated legislation will further simplify the terms and definitions as dictated by the state. Mr. Illuminati also acknowledged the extensive work session previously reviewed by the Planning Commission to produce the County legislation regulating solar projects before state law superseded the County. 

Chairman Dashiell thanked Mr. Illuminati for reviewing the Text Amendment. With no additional comments from the Commissioners, Chairman Dashiell welcomed public comments during the Public Hearing for the Text Amendment review. 

Mr. Nathanial Morton, who lives on Union Church Road, approached the podium. Addressing the Commissioners, he stated that he was directly affected by a Public Service Commission Certificate of Public Convenience and Necessity (CPCN), Case number 9795, which was finally ordered six weeks before this meeting. 

With respect to the County’s participation in the CPCN proceeding, Mr. Morton stressed the importance of representation from local interests during these proceedings. While he agrees with a lot of items listed in the Text Amendment, Mr. Morton proposed additional items for the Commissioners’ consideration as follows: 

- Wicomico County should not only attend the public hearings, but should intervene in every CPCN hearing to consider public comments. This gives impacted citizens ample notice. 

- The County should establish a parallel notification advisory board within this commission at the time of application filing, before notifying affected homeowners. The County should also provide notice at the beginning stages of the application review at the proceedings. 

- Every CPCN application should receive a full, coordinated review by the Department of Law and a multidisciplinary team, not just the items the County covers for the State. 

- Though the landscaping, bonding code, noise, and site plan provisions in the amendment regulate aspects of the development, they do not establish how the county will independently examine the factual and technical record behind the public service approval.

###### **The Public Hearing ended at 4:46 p.m.** 

Chairman Dashiell welcomed comments from the Commissioners, and Mr. Moreno-Holt asked for technical explanations to better understand the Text Amendment and public comments. 

Mr. Illuminati reiterated Mr. Morton’s points that the requirement of the Planning Commission to attend all meetings and hearings is different from legal interventions. While old state law cited solar utility approval for facilities 5 megawatts or larger, the current state law cites that solar utility is larger than one megawatt. Also worth noting is that the State regulates the noise regulations, but they can be explored in detail. 

In light of public and Commissioners’ comments, Mr. Illuminati suggested tabling formal approval of the Text Amendment until the next meeting, though another public hearing is not necessary. Both Chairman Dashiell and Ms. Bynum-King agreed to table the vote on the Text Amendment until the following meeting. Ms. Justice stated there were no concerns with postponing a decision until Mr. Illuminati could research the Text Amendment regarding public comments. 

As there were no additional comments, Chairman Dashiell requested a motion to table the legislation until the next meeting, to allow Mr. Illuminati’s research based on the public comments, and to formally close the Public Hearing. 

Mr. Moreno Holt moved to table the legislation until the next meeting, which was seconded by Mr. Copeland, and the motion duly carried. 

Chairman Dashiell stated the motion was **APPROVED.**

Ms. Thayne and Mr. Rob Davis, represented by Plummer and Associates, approached the table. Ms. Thayne presented the Staff Report. 

The minor subdivision plat for the lands of Michael and Diane Bloxham proposes creating one new lot from its parent parcel. A similar minor subdivision plat was presented earlier this year, and subdivision regulations allow the parent parcel to be subdivided to create three lots following the regulatory date in April 1968 without full Planning Commission review. For this minor subdivision, lot one has an existing single-family dwelling and an approved septic reserve area. Lot two has an existing driveway. This plat created an easement for shared driveway access for lots one and two. Lot one meets all the zoning and subdivision standards, including minimum lot size. The subdivision plat has Health Department and Department of Public Works approval, along with Forest Conservation Exemption approval. 

Planning staff recommends approval of the final draft of the plat as presented with the following three (3) conditions:

###### <u>Summary of Proposal:</u> 

This plat proposes the creation of two new lots from a 71.5-acre parcel shown as Parcel 54 on Tax Map 50 (see Attachments 1 and 2).  The lots created by this plat, lots three and four, will be 5.95 acres and 2.00 acres, respectively. These new lots will be the third and fourth lots subdivided from the parent parcel since the regulatory date of April 1, 1968. The previous subdivisions met the criteria of §2003,“Exclusions,” in the Subdivision Section of Wicomico’s County Code, which allows for three total lots to be created from the parent parcel since the regulatory date without adhering to the full requirements of Chapter 200 and the density requirements found in Table §225-74, “Schedule of Maximum Permitted Residential Densities.” The subdivision proposed with this plat will create the fourth lot from  the parent parcel since the County’s regulatory date and therefore requires Planning Commission approval. This fourth lot is also subject to the aforementioned density requirements for the same reason, and must

### Chapter 4 – Housing 

Housing is a fundamental human need and a critical component of a healthy, resilient, and sustainable community. The availability of safe, quality, and affordable housing is essential to Wicomico County’s long-term economic vitality, social wellbeing, and overall quality of life. By fostering investment in homes and neighborhoods throughout the County, this Comprehensive Plan supports strong communities, reinforces local character and identity, and promotes civic pride through a diverse range of housing opportunities. 

Through this chapter, Wicomico County affirms its commitment to planning for affordable, workforce, and diverse housing opportunities that meet the needs of current and future residents. The County also reaffirms its dedication to advancing fair housing through the goals, objectives, policies, and implementation strategies contained within this Housing Element and Comprehensive Plan. Wicomico County will continue to coordinate with the Maryland Department of Planning, the Maryland Department of Housing and Community Development, municipalities within the County, housing providers, and community stakeholders to support fair housing planning efforts and housing initiatives. This chapter examines existing housing conditions throughout Wicomico County, identifies current and projected housing needs, and evaluates housing trends and challenges that will influence the County’s future growth and development.

##### **<mark>GUIDING PRINCIPLES AND LEGISLATION</mark>** 

The housing vision found in Maryland’s Land Use Article §3-114 recommends providing a range of housing densities, types, and sizes for citizens of all ages and incomes. This vision sets the baseline for the County to provide for sufficient, affordable workforce housing along with a full range of housing options that meet the needs of all citizens. This vision is supplemented by requirements identified in HB 1045, which emphasizes adequately providing local, affordable workforce housing. These guidelines are the basis for the housing information and analysis presented in this chapter. 

###### **Fair Housing Act** 

The Fair Housing Act is a federal law that protects people from discrimination when they are renting, buying, securing financing for housing, or engaging in other housing related activities. The prohibitions specifically cover discrimination because of race, color, national origin, religion, sex, disability, and the presence of children. The Act covers most housing. In very rare circumstances, the Act exempts owner-occupied buildings with no more than four units, single-family houses sold or rented by the owner without the use of an agent, and housing operated by religious organizations and private clubs that limit occupancy to members. 

###### **Land Use Article** 

The Maryland Land Use Article §3-114 describes the requirements for a comprehensive plan’s housing element. It requires that, among other components, the element addresses the need for affordable housing, including workforce housing and low-income housing. In this context, low-income housing is housing that is affordable for a household with an annual income that is below 60% of Area Median Income (AMI). Workforce housing includes rental housing that is affordable for a household with an annual income that is 50-100% of AMI, homeownership housing that is affordable to a household with annual income that is 60-120% of AMI, or in recognized Maryland Mortgage Program target areas, affordable to a household with an annual income that is 60-150% of AMI. 

###### **Rental Housing Code** 

The Rental Housing Code establishes a legal framework for maintaining safe, healthy, and habitable rental properties within the County. It requires landlords to obtain a rental license, submit to periodic inspections, and comply with standards for building maintenance, occupancy, and safety. This code is important because it helps protect tenants from substandard living conditions, ensures accountability among property owners, and supports the overall quality of housing in the community. By enforcing these standards, the city promotes public health, neighborhood stability, and a fair housing environment.

###### **HB 1045** 

As discussed in **Chapter 1** , House Bill 1045 requires Maryland jurisdictions to include a Housing Element in their comprehensive plans. This chapter fulfills that requirement by evaluating local housing needs and identifying policies and actions to support a range of housing opportunities for current and future residents. 

###### **HB 90** 

As discussed in **Chapter 1** , House Bill 90 established additional fair housing requirements and emphasizes the importance of affirmatively furthering fair housing. 

HB 90 defines affirmatively furthering fair housing as, taking meaningful actions to: 

- Overcome patterns of segregation 

- Foster inclusive communities free from barriers that restrict access to housing and opportunity based on protected characteristics 

- Address significant disparities in housing needs and access to opportunity 

- Replace segregated living patterns with truly integrated and balanced living patterns 

- Foster and maintain compliance with civil rights and fair housing laws 

This Housing Element has been developed considering these legislative changes and requirements. 

##### **<mark>HOUSING DEMAND AND SUPPLY</mark>** 

The Wicomico County Housing Study 2026 provides a snapshot of the county's population and housing stock outside the City of Salisbury. The study indicates that Wicomico County's population grew moderately from 81,564 residents in 2000 to 101,838 residents in 2025. Population growth has been uneven across the county. District 5 (Parsons) remains the most populous district and experienced the largest increase, growing by approximately 5.2 percent, from 19,764 to 25,455 residents. District 9 (Salisbury) grew by roughly 3 percent, while several rural districts, including Quantico, experienced population declines. 

Wicomico County's housing inventory consists of approximately 43,383 housing units, with an overall occupancy rate of 92 percent. Recent residential development has been concentrated in the Parsons and Camden districts. Between 2020 and 2025, the county added approximately 1,204 net new housing units, representing modest growth in the overall housing supply.<sup>1</sup>

Since residential development has generally followed population growth patterns, areas experiencing slower growth have seen comparatively less housing investment. The 2026 Housing Study found that, although most homes in the county remain in stable exterior condition, housing distress is concentrated in specific neighborhoods and is closely linked to aging housing stock, long-term disinvestment, and limited reinvestment capacity. The Westside area and several secondary neighborhoods exhibit disproportionately high housing needs, highlighting the importance of targeted revitalization and reinvestment efforts. 

The study also identifies a broader structural challenge within the local housing market. The county's existing housing supply, including its pricing, location, and mix of unit types, no longer fully align with the household incomes, sizes, or life stages of residents looking to buy or rent.  Detached single-family homes continue to dominate the housing inventory even as the number of smaller households, renters, seniors, and workforce households has increased. As a result, the county faces persistent shortages of affordable and workforce rental housing, limited availability of deeply affordable units, and increasing pressure on the existing stock of naturally occurring affordable housing. In many cases, the housing supply available does not adequately meet the needs, preferences, or financial circumstances of current and future residents. 

To address evolving demographic trends and housing needs, the County must ensure an adequate supply of land designated for residential development while encouraging a broader range of housing options. Maintaining a balance between housing demand and supply requires housing choices that accommodate households at different income levels, life stages, and housing preferences. Future housing strategies should promote a diverse mix of housing types, support appropriate residential densities, provide flexibility in location choices, and encourage new development near employment centers, services, schools, and other community amenities. Because residential construction is largely driven by the private sector, Wicomico County should continue working collaboratively with builders and developers to expand housing opportunities and support a diverse mix of housing in urban, suburban, and exurban areas.

Recent residential development has continued but at a more moderate pace, with 4,712 units (10.7%) built since 2010, including 634 units (1.4%) constructed in 2020 or later. 

Older housing remains a significant component of the County’s overall housing inventory. In total, 19,345 units (43.8%) were constructed before 1980. Housing units constructed before 1976 are now at least 50 years old and meet the age threshold for potential consideration on the National Register of Historic Places (NRHP). Because ACS data groups housing units by decade, the data indicate that at least 29.7% of the County's housing stock, specifically those units built in 1969 or earlier, meet this threshold. Additionally, some housing units included within the 1970-1979 category were undoubtedly constructed prior to 1976 and may also exceed the 50-year threshold, although the ACS does not provide sufficient detail to determine the exact number. 

The substantial presence of older housing throughout Wicomico County underscores the importance of policies and programs that support housing rehabilitation, historic preservation, and neighborhood reinvestment. Continued efforts to identify grant opportunities, financing tools, and technical assistance programs can help preserve the County's older housing stock while ensuring these homes remain safe, functional, and attractive for current and future residents.

###### **<mark>HOUSING COSTS</mark>** 

Housing costs often account for a significant portion of a household budget and can affect the lives of residents in a variety of ways. Homeowners who want to build equity would like to see the value of their investment increase to maximize their financial outcomes. Renters, on the other hand, may see their expenses rise over time as rents increase. Whether homeowner or renter, households that struggle to afford housing costs find themselves in unstable positions that can affect their quality of life. Municipalities and counties also have a stake in the cost and value of housing within their borders as it can affect the community’s overall economic health. 

Housing affordability has become an increasing concern both regionally and locally as housing costs continue to rise faster than household incomes. A common measure of affordability is the percentage of household income spent on housing, with housing generally considered affordable when costs do not exceed 30 percent of a household's income. Households that spend more than 30 percent of their income on housing are considered “cost-burdened”, and those exceeding 50 percent face “severe cost burden.”

Homeowners with a mortgage spend approximately $19,848 annually on housing costs, or 26.0 percent of the median household income, also within acceptable range since it does not exceed the general 30 percent marker.<sup>2</sup>

###### **Workforce Housing** 

Workforce housing is housing affordable to households earning between 60-120 percent of AMI. Workforce housing targets middle-income workers, which includes professions such as police officers, firefighters, teachers, healthcare workers, and retail workers. Those who need workforce housing may not always qualify for subsidized housing programs such as the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC) program or the Housing Choice Vouchers program (formerly known as Section 8). 

From the 1940s to the 1990s, housing was generally affordable to many middle-income workers due to wages remaining relatively consistent with costs of living. During the late 1990s and early 2000s, incomes began to lag behind rising costs of living, and housing supply for middle-income workers grew stagnant, causing an acute need for workforce housing. Because affordable housing programs focused on serving households making 60 percent or lower of AMI, middle-income workers were left with fewer housing options. 

Federal programs through HUD or state governments are predominantly focused on supporting people that make less than 60 percent of AMI. The workforce housing target of 60-120 percent of AMI is an income stratum that is largely unserved and unaddressed by both federal and state programs aside from FHA loans. Affordable housing for the working and middle classes is largely left to individual municipalities and counties to deal with. Families that fall into this income category have found it increasingly difficult to purchase a home that is located in the area where they work and provides for the needs of their household. In response, many families have found housing that is significantly further away from their place of employment. This results in increased commute times, greater reliance on transportation infrastructure, less free time, and ultimately lower quality of life.

###### **<mark>FAIR HOUSING ASSESSMENT</mark>** 

Wicomico County is committed to affirmatively furthering fair housing in accordance with the federal Fair Housing Act and Maryland House Bill 90 (2021). This assessment evaluates local housing conditions to identify patterns of segregation, disparities in access to housing and opportunity, and barriers affecting protected classes. The analysis draws upon data presented throughout this chapter, including U.S. Census Bureau and American Community Survey (ACS) data, local housing and demographic trends, and findings from housing studies and stakeholder engagement efforts. Together, these sources provide a comprehensive understanding of housing conditions, demographic patterns, and inequities across Wicomico County. 

###### **Demographic Patterns and Protected Classes** 

Wicomico County is one of the most diverse jurisdictions on Maryland's Eastern Shore and has a significantly higher proportion of racial and ethnic minority residents than many neighboring counties. Black or African American residents represent a substantial share of Wicomico County's population, accounting for approximately 29 percent of residents<sup>6</sup> , with concentrations in portions of Salisbury, Fruitland, Delmar, and several rural communities. Hispanic and Latino populations have also grown steadily over the past two decades, contributing to the county's increasing diversity. 

Wicomico County is also experiencing demographic changes associated with an aging population. While educational institutions and employment centers continue to attract younger adults, the number of older residents aged 65 and older has increased, now representing 19 percent of the population.<sup>7</sup> These trends create a growing need for accessible, adaptable, and age-friendly housing options that allow older adults and persons with disabilities to remain in their communities. Ensuring that housing opportunities are available to residents of all ages, abilities, races, ethnicities, and household types is an important fair housing consideration.

Housing affordability represents one of the most significant fair housing challenges in Wicomico County. Rising housing costs have increased financial pressure on both renters and prospective homebuyers, particularly low- and moderate-income households. - Habitat for Humanity Critical Repairs & Accessibility – offers repairs/modifications for seniors and disabled homeowners. 

- Maryland Special Loans Program (SLP) – state loans for rehabilitation and lead hazard reduction. 

- Weatherization Assistance Program (DHCD) – provides energy ‑ efficiency upgrades for low ‑ income households. 

Serves homeowners (often seniors or disabled) earning below 80 % AMI. Funding comes from CDBG, state loans or nonprofit fundraising. Demand exceeds resources, suggesting the need to expand capacity. 

###### **Development Programs** 

- USDA Section 515 Rural Rental Housing – direct loans to develop or preserve affordable rental housing in rural areas (excluding Salisbury). 

- USDA Multi ‑ Family Housing Preservation – helps recapitalize aging USDA ‑ financed projects.

- LIHTC Development – federal tax credits administered by Maryland DHCD to encourage private development of affordable rental units. 

Target rural rental projects or larger multi ‑ family developments. LIHTC projects require a local contribution or supportive policies to be competitive. 

###### **Homelessness Services** 

- Rapid Re ‑ Housing (Lower Shore Continuum of Care – CoC) – short ‑ term rental assistance and case management. 

- Permanent Supportive Housing – rental assistance with supportive services for chronically homeless individuals. 

- Emergency Shelter/Transitional Housing – run by nonprofits and faith ‑ based groups. 

- Street Outreach & Case Management – Lower Shore CoC operates outreach and supportive services. 

Programs serve people experiencing homelessness; eligibility based on HUD definitions of homelessness. Funding comes from HUD’s Emergency Solutions Grant (ESG), Continuum of Care grants and local donations. 

###### **Past & Pilot Programs** 

- COVID ‑ 19 Emergency Rental Assistance – provided short ‑ term rent and utility relief during the pandemic; currently inactive. 

- CARES Act Emergency Solutions Grant (ESG ‑ CV) – expanded shelter capacity and services; funding ended. 

- Housing Trust Fund (concept) – considered but not yet established; would create local dedicated funds for affordable housing. 

- Rental Registration & Inspection (concept) – a proposed program to register and inspect rentals to improve quality. 

- Workforce Housing Development Partnerships (concept) – exploration of employer ‑ assisted housing initiatives. 

Pilot programs highlight potential tools (local trust fund, rental registry, employer partnerships) that could be adopted. Emergency rental assistance demonstrated the value of flexible funding. 

###### **Federal & State Funding Sources** 

- Community Development Block Grant (CDBG) – formula grant from HUD; Wicomico currently uses it for housing rehabilitation and infrastructure; could be reallocated to fund repairs or gap financing.
