Public Hearing: Resolution No. 129-2025 — To amend the 2010 Wicomico County Water and Sewer Plan for the City of Fruitland Water System to provide for the withdraw of water from the Manokin Aquifer via the installation of two additional confined aquifer supply wells, the addition of treatment for emerging contaminants (PEAS), and to abandon four existing unconfined supply wells in that system. Mr. Cannon opened the Public Hearing. There were no public comments. Mr. Cannon closed the Public Hearing. 

Mr. Adam Corry, Executive Director of Environmental Services, and Ms. Katherine McAllister of George, Miles and Buhr came before Council. Mr. Corry said tonight we wanted to bring to you this Resolution, which is relatively straightforward. He said this is for the City of Fruitland to be able to upgrade current wells in the wellfield that are in the Columbia aquifer. He said the Columbia aquifer is an unconfined aquifer. He said you may remember that Fruitland some time ago had some issues with the Morris Mill subdivision with the trichlorethylene plume, and that is actually a project I worked on when I was still with MDE, one of the upgrades that was done in 2020, and then the addition of a Manokin aquifer confined well. He said this is just going the next step, but this is for the protection of PFAS and PFOS. 

Mr. Ennis asked Mr. Corry to explain how confined and unconfined it is, to which Mr. Corry responded, think of our geology on the Eastern Shore like a Smith Island cake — all of those layers represent time. He said alluvial deposits that were washed down from glaciers that were taken from the Piedmonts and the Appalachian Range and laid down layer upon layer upon layer. He said when you have shallow aquifers, something that’s superficial and comes back every year like your wet season water table that could be anywhere from two feet below ground to 15-20 feet below ground, that’s a real shallow well. He said then you get into aquifers like the Columbia that are a little bit deeper, for example, and you get into the 100 foot category or maybe even 65 feet, but it still has hydraulic communication with the shallow young water that is over the top of it, so it is susceptible to surface contamination — nitrates from agricultural practices and other old practices and sewage systems, and then of course solvents and other

chemicals that are sinkers like TCE goes down. He said PEAS is pretty prevalent once it gets into the groundwater, so it keeps moving on down. He said at some point down there in that Smith Island cake you get to the real yummy chocolate part, and we’re going to pretend that chocolate part is what’s called an aquiclude or an aquitard, and that actually prevents that surface water from communicating with it, so that water is much older. He said typically, that aquifer is starting somewhere way out there on the Piedmont or Appalachian Range and it dips down underneath the Chesapeake Bay, and that water is going to be safer in theory. He said when a well is constructed in a confined aquifer, you always grout that section off in your annular space on the outside of your well casing, and that way, you don’t get any upper communication down effectively comingling those two bodies of water together. 

Ms. Shields asked, is the contamination affecting any of the residents in Fruitland, to which Ms. McAllister responded, it is fairly low in the levels. She said the State has fully funded this project, so right now, to answer your question, yes, those surface aquifer wells do have PEAS in them, but it’s a very, very low concentration. 

Mr. Merritt said the water treatment center in Fruitland is going to have to be expanded.
