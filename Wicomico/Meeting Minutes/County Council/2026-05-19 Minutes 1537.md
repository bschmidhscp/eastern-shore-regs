Ms. Gray transitioned to a review of several recent state and local health initiatives beginning with
the AHEAD model, which stands for Achieving Health Care Efficiency Through Accountable Design.
She explained that it is
a Center
forMedicare and Medicaid Services waiver program that allows
states and health care providers flexibility in testing new models and goals toward achieving major
objectives. She said those objectives include improving population health and decreasing the
burden of disease, expanding primary care to increase access for residents, incentivizing hospitals
and specialists for the quality of care they provide, and reducing the burden of health care costs for
taxpayers and residents. She explained that waiver programs are not novel in Maryland and have
been around since the late 1970s, starting with a program that lasted until 2013 allowing the state
to set its own rate and cost controls for hospitals. This was expanded into an all-payer model in
2014, which essentially established global budgets for hospitals to encourage quality over quantity
and discourage increased volume simply to generate revenue from avoidable, preventable
hospitalizations. The state then moved into a total cost of care model, which acknowledged that
the whole continuum of care includes primary care facilities, skilled nursing facilities, and other
health professionals and the interplay between those systems. The current iteration—the AHEAD
model—launched in 2026, and requires reporting on a Population Health Accountability Plan, or
PHAP. She noted that CMS required Maryland to select six population health measures to monitor
over the next 10-year cycle, with targets for each domain area. The measures come from two major
data sources: the Behavioral Health Risk Factor Surveillance System, which is a randomized
annual survey of adults focusing on risk behaviors, chronic health conditions, and preventive
services; and an all-payer claims database, which aggregates medical, dental, and pharmacy
claims across private and public insurers.

Self-Reported Health Status: Ms. Gray reported that currently, 16.5 percent of Maryland residents
and 17.7 percent of Wicomico residents report fair or poor health in the past year, a trend that has
worsened slightly over recent years both statewide and in the county. She said the AHEAD target is
16.5 percent or lower by 2036. She noted that the latest Behavioral Health Risk Factor Surveillance
System data found that upward of one in four Hispanic adults are reporting fair or poor health
status — approximately 25 percent compared to an overall value of 16 percent. She said the
county's Community Health Improvement Plan, a partnership with TidalHealth and Somerset
County Health Department, addresses strategies around improving health through enhanced
community health worker workforce, strengthening electronic health referrals, retaining medical
residents in the area, and reducing stigma around accessing behavioral health services.
Colorectal Cancer Screening: Ms. Gray reported that currently, 49.3 percent of eligible Maryland
residents and 47 percent of eligible Wicomico residents had reported at least one screening in the
past year, with CMS seeking to raise that to 54.6 percent by the end of the period. She said the
overall trend shows really strong positive growth for both the county and the state, noting that in
2018, only one in three eligible adults in the county had reported a recent screening, and that the
county is now up to 47 percent. She said overall incidence and mortality from colon cancer had
fallen over the past five years in the county, but she wanted to spotlight younger age groups. She
said the American Cancer Society produced an analysis in March reporting that colorectal cancers
are the leading cause of cancer death among those 50 years and younger, and that while overall
mortality has decreased, that improvement is really driven by older age groups. She said the
incidence of colorectal cancers in those 20-49 years of age has increased three percent annually
from 2013 to 2022. She noted that over half of colorectal cancers are attributable to modifiable risk
factors such as smoking status, unhealthy diets low in fiber, higher alcohol consumption, physical
inactivity, and obesity. She said locally, the Health Department has already exceeded its annual
goal of 45 screenings through only three quarters of the fiscal year, having completed 70 and
counting, compared to 47 in the prior fiscal year. She said those with positive screens are being
sustained through case management while they await care, so as not to lose anyone to follow-up.

Hemoglobin A1C Control: Ms. Gray explained that poor control of A1C leads to severe
complications including vision loss, heart disease, kidney damage, and neuropathy. She said two-
thirds of Maryland residents with diabetes had an A1C of 9 or higher during 2023, with a statewide
target to reduce that to 60.3 percent. She said the good news is that Wicomico County is well
within that target — less than half of diabetic residents in the relevant age group, specifically 43
percent, had poor control during that same period, which she said was actually the second best
value she saw in the state. She said that while older age groups show about 1 in 3 with an A1C
above that value, when younger age groups are examined, 2 out of every 3 in the 18-30 age group
have poor control, making that another area for outreach focus.
Mr. Winn asked whether this is type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Ms. Gray responded, both, combined. Mr.

Gray responded, there is not really a good data source for that at this point, noting that the last self-
reported diabetes value for the county is about 13 percent overall. She offered to try to find out the
breakdown between type 1 and type 2.
Ms. Gray said locally, chronic disease is an entire priority area for the county's Community Health
improvement Plan, including expanding diabetes prevention programs in multiple languages noting
that Wicomico is one of the only areas to offer that in Haitian Creole. She said they are also
expanding bidirectional electronic referrals through the CRISP system to get more people into
diabetes education and management classes throughout the county.
Follow-Up Post Mental Illness Hospitalization: Ms. Gray explained that this measure looks at the
critical 7-day and 30-day post-discharge follow-ups for primary mental illness conditions among
those 18 and older, with follow-ups defined as telehealth visits, residential placements, outpatient
visits with a behavioral heaith provider, and/or partial hospitalizations. She said the good news is
that both the state and the county are increasing these rates over the past few years. She reported
that Wicomico is almost right at the target for the 30-day level at 66.7 percent compared to the
CMS target of 66.8 percent. She said there is a wider gap at the acute 7-day follow-up mark, with
the county sitting at 36.5 percent, working toward the 40.5 percent mark. She noted a distinct
disparity by race and ethnicity in the county, with 3 in 4 white non-Hispanic residents receiving
follow-up within 30 days, while that percentage drops to a little over half for Hispanic and Black
non-Hispanic residents. She said this measure is also included in the most recent Health
Department's local behavioral health authority strategic plan and is a possible area of expansion
under the Rural Health Transformation Program.

Food Insecurity: Ms. Gray described this as a worsening trend in the state and county. She said

Ms. Gray introduced the Rural Health Transformation Program (RHDP) as another population
health initiative recently started in Maryland. She said overall, it is a federal $50 billion-dollar fund
authorized by HR1 last July to strengthen rural communities. She said in December 2025, Maryland
was awarded $168 million dollars of that federal funding, renewable over 5 years. She noted that 18
of Maryland's 24 jurisdictions are considered rural, including Wicomico County.
Ms. Gray said the program focuses on three key pillars. The first is to expand existing and
implement new efforts to develop, recruit, and retain a strong health workforce in rural
communities, addressing multiple types of health care clinicians and allied health professionals.
She said earmarked programs for the state could include expansion of area health education
centers, which are hubs for community health workers, expanding apprenticeship and training
programs for allied health programs, and recruitment and retention of physicians and advanced
practice professionals in rural communities.
Ms. Gray said the second pillar is expanding existing and implementing new efforts to bring health
care services into rural areas, including expansion of primary care services and trauma responses,
strengthening health information exchange infrastructure and connectivity, support for regional
integrated mobile health programs, and the expansion of behavioral health services.
Ms. Gray said the third pillar is focused on empowering rural Marylanders to eat for health,
increasing access to nutritious, locally grown and raised Maryland foods through nutritional
education and the establishment of a rural food coordination program throughout the state.
Ms. Gray said the anticipated focus in budget period one for local Health Departments is on pillar
two, specifically the promotion of access and innovative care through mobile health expansion.
She said one of the areas the Health Department would like to explore is an expansion of the
county's Salisbury Wicomico Integrated First Care Team, or SWIFT, a mobile health initiative
between TidalHealth and Salisbury Fire Department. She described SWIFT as a fantastic initiative
featuring an interdisciplinary team of a nurse practitioner, registered nurse, paramedics, social

screening for the lower shore, making it also a priority of this initiative.
Ms. Gray said she also wanted to briefly touch on the latest Youth Risk Behavior Survey results for
the county, noting they were very recently released and that she would provide a high-level
overview. She said similarto the Behavioral Health Risk Factor Surveillance System for adults, the
Youth Risk Behavior Survey looks at self-reported risk among Maryland middle and high school
students and is conducted every other year. She said there are six major areas: alcohol and drug
use, tobacco use, injury and violence, weight management and physical activity, sexual behaviors,
and other state-added miscellaneous topics.
Ms. Gray said overall, there were far more improving statistics than those that appeared static or
worsening. On substance use, she reported less alcohol use at earlier ages for high schoolers, as
well as fewer students reporting current alcohol and/or binge drinking. She said fewer middle and
high school students also reported currently using marijuana or ever using other illicit drugs,
including heroin, methamphetamines, ecstasy, and cocaine. She said middle and high schoolers in
the county are also reporting less current and early-age cigarette, cigar, and smokeless tobacco
use, and specifically, cigarette use has seen a very dramatic decrease. She said in 2013, the same

2.7 percent. She acknowledged that electronic vape use or smoking devices remain popular and
said high schoolers are starting to showa slight drop-off in that, while middle schoolers show no
change. She noted that the enticement still appears to be flavored vape use versus traditional
tobacco, and said their outreach programs focus on those topics.
On injury and violence, Ms. Gray said all students reported they were less likely to ride with a driver
who had been drinking or texting, and that the key mental health metrics relating
to feelings of
