##### **A.** **<u>OVERVIEW:</u>** 

On April 4, 2025, the Department of Technical and Community Services received an application from Theodore Miller, requesting a rezoning of his property (Tax Map 23, Grid 3, Parcel 2) from Residential (‘R-2’) to Agricultural Residential (‘AR’) citing a mistake in the existing Zoning Classification. The parcel is approximately 72.47 Acres in size, is located near Princess Anne, on the southern side of Brownstone Rd. Mr. Miller has indicated that he would like to continue his farming operation by applying to the Maryland Agricultural Land Preservation Foundation (MALPF) for a conservation easement. **A public hearing on the rezoning request has been scheduled and noticed for the Planning Commission’s July 10, 2025 meeting.** 

_*For rezoning applications, the Planning Commission will need to hold a Public Hearing on the request and make a recommendation to the County Commissioners. The County Commissioners must also hold a Public Hearing before approving or denying the request._ 

##### **B.** **<u>RELEVENT BACKGROUND INFORMATION:</u>** 

**1.** **<u>Existing and Proposed Land Use:</u>** The current land use is farmland. There is some woodland on the property and to the south is Manokin River. Mr. Miller proposes to continue using the property as agricultural land in perpetuity by applying for a conservation easement. 

2. **<u>Neighborhood:</u>** For rezoning applications, Maryland Case Law requires that a ‘neighborhood’ be identified. Neighborhoods are usually bordered by geographic features such as roads or streams, and in rural areas, can be larger than neighborhoods in urban or

- **C.** **<u>REZONING CRITERIA:</u>** The Maryland Land Use Article provides criteria to be considered for rezoning applications.  These items are listed below and are also addressed in the attached recommendation. 

   1. **<u>Population Change:</u>** In 2010, the total population of Somerset County was 26,470 persons. By 2020, this had decreased to 24,620 according to the United States Census Bureau. The most recent American Community Survey (ACS) estimates that the total population is roughly 24,910 representing an approximate 6 % change in population from 2000 to 2018. Within the identified neighborhood, population change is estimated to be minimal since the last comprehensive rezoning in 2019. 

   2. **<u>Availability of Public Facilities:</u>** Sewer and water are not available on this property. Transportation access is available via Deal Island Road (State Route 363). 

   3. **<u>Present and Future Transportation Patterns:</u>** Traffic volumes on Deal Island Road in the vicinity of the applicant’s property have decreased since the last County Comprehensive rezoning in 2019. The most recent numbers available shown from the AADT range from 2014 to 2022. Those numbers decreased from 3,471 vehicles per day in 2014, to 3,461 vehicles per day in 2022. Currently, the site sees minimal traffic on Brownstone as it serves as an access/exit road for a small subdivision and a residential street, with direct access to Deal Island Road. 

   4. **<u>Compatibility with Existing and Proposed Development for the Area</u>** <u>: In general,</u> rezoning’s can occur with the express purpose of using the land for another type of development. For example, a rezoning from Agricultural Residential to Commercial might occur in order to allow for the development of a grocery store. With Mr. Miller’s request, he intends to continue using the land in its current capacity, but just under a different zoning designation. As the current land use is agricultural and the proposed use is agricultural, the Planning Commission should find that the proposed land use is compatible with the existing land uses in the neighborhood. 

   5. **<u>Relationship of the Proposed Amendment to the Comprehensive Plan</u>** : The Future Land Use Map suggests that this parcel be residential in nature. Surrounding properties are also intended to be residential and ‘mixed use/infill.’ Undeniably, there is a conflict with the proposed rezoning due to the directives set by the Comprehensive Plan back in 1996.

6. **<u>Change in the Character of the Neighborhood</u>** :  In accordance with State Law, a rezoning can only be approved by a finding that there has been a “substantial change” in the character of the neighborhood since the last comprehensive rezoning, or that there is a “mistake” in the existing zoning (see #7 below). **The last comprehensive rezoning occurred in 2019, so any changes to justify the rezoning will need to have occurred since that date.** Staff found that since 2019 the property immediately to the north of this property has been rezoned Agricultural Residential. 

   - **<u>Mistake in the Existing Zoning</u>** <u>: This property was zoned R-2 during the County’s</u> last comprehensive rezoning in 2019, having been originally rezoned as R-2 from A- R in 2001. During the 2019 rezoning, the argument could be made that the parcel incorrectly identified as an opportunity for residential development, despite its agricultural nature and historical use. 

The 2019 rezoning wrongfully relied on outdated future land use maps from the 1996 Comprehensive Plan, which planned for residential growth that ultimately did not occur. Further, the 2001 rezoning, likely occurred due to projected and desired residential growth in the county. With the 2008 recession along with other factors, residential growth never materialized in the area, while agricultural use persisted as the primary use of land in this area. Currently, staff believes there was an error in the methodology used for the 2019 rezoning. 

Staff feel it is worth noting, that since 2019 there has been one successful rezoning from R-2 to A-R of the adjoining parcel to the north of the applicant.

The current land use is agricultural with all future plans provided by Mr. Miller indicating that the property will continue to be farmed along with an application for a conservation easement on the property. Staff is confident that Mr. Miller anticipates applying for the MALPF program (if approved for the rezoning). Based on current population estimates for the County (suggesting loss of population), it is unlikely to be developed residentially for the foreseeable future. ##### **Chapter 13 Somerset Villages and Small Area Plans** 

##### _13.1 Princess Anne/Westover vicinity_ 

Princess Anne is Somerset County’s second largest town, with a 1990 population of approximately 1,700 in the town limits, and a further 2,100 in the surrounding area. This includes West Princess Anne, the Stewarts Neck Road area and the University of Maryland Eastern Shore. There are a further 1,500 persons at the Eastern Shore Correctional Institute (ECI). 

Princess Anne was founded in 1773. It is the administrative center for County government and is the focal point of most of the County’s business activities, social and transportation services. Located near the headwaters of the Manokin River, the town was originally an important trading center for the agricultural products of the Eastern Shore, and is still served by rail from Wilmington, Delaware on the Conrail system. US 13 by-passes the town to the west enroute from Philadelphia to Norfolk, and carries over 19,000 vehicles per day in the Princess Anne vicinity. 

The lands east of the Town are generally well-drained and productive farmland. To the west are the meandering channels of the Manokin River, Taylor Branch, Hall Branch, and Jones Creek, with their associated Critical Areas and floodplains. Development opportunities are more limited to the west; however, recent residential growth has sought out these areas served by Stewarts Neck Road, Mount Vernon Road (MD362) and Deal Island Road (MD 363). Recent annexations by the Town of Princess Anne have centered on residential and commercial areas west of US13, along with some areas to the north and east of the town. 

Commercial development has stayed close to the US13/Conrail corridor. The County’s first industrial/office park is located south of Princess Anne, and the entire east side of US13 as far south as Westover is currently zoned industrial, although this area has not so far seen much actual development. 

Princess Anne’s historic district is well-known, along with several significant National Register structures. The ‘Olde Princess Anne Days’ festival and historic house tour have been held in the fall since 1958. The publicity surrounding this event should be expanded to attract visitors to Princess Anne throughout the year. This, together with the Crisfield/Smith Island tours, form the backbone of Somerset County’s tourism industry. 

The Princess Anne area is served by water and sewer systems operated by the County Sanitary District. Three wells and two storage tanks provide water to a population of 3,800, and two additional storage tanks will serve the ECI prison population of 2,480. The system has the capacity to serve an additional 11,500 population, or 4,500 dwelling units. The sewerage system includes eight pumping stations and a main treatment plant. It currently serves a population of 3,800 and has capacity for 3,200 more, or 1,260 dwelling units. The

##### **RECOMMENDATIONS** 

**Three growth nodes are recommended for the Princess Anne area: Deal Island Road to the west, the UMES campus area to the north-east, and Stewarts Neck Road to the south-east. ###### **Maryland Department of Planning Review Comments Somerset County 2025 Draft Plan Amendment** 

The Maryland Department of Planning (MDP) received the draft 2025 Somerset County Comprehensive Plan Amendment (Draft Plan) from the County on April 10, 2025. These comments are offered as suggestions to improve the Draft Plan and better address the statutory requirements of the Land Use Article. Other state agencies, as noted below, have contributed comments and others may submit comments separately. If comments are subsequently received by MDP, the department will forward them to the county. 

###### **Maryland Planning Principles** 

The Land Use Article <u>Section 1-201</u> requires Maryland jurisdictions with planning & zoning authority to implement the state’s 12 Planning Visions (Visions) through the comprehensive plan. During the 2025 session, the Maryland General Assembly adopted SB 266, which replaces the Visions with the 8 Sustainable Growth Planning Principles (Principles) but maintains the requirement for the Principles to be implemented in comprehensive plans. This change is effective October 1, 2025. In partnership with other state agencies and local governments, MDP is developing guidance to help jurisdictions craft and implement comprehensive plans adopted after October 1 that address the new Principles. During the spring and summer of 2025, MDP is conducting a series of regional roundtables to solicit local recommendations and requests related to Principles implementation. MDP will use roundtable insight to inform guidance development. 

MDP acknowledges that Somerset County planned for and completed the Draft 2025 Somerset County Comprehensive Plan Amendment under the direction of current Maryland planning law, focused on implementing the Visions. The applicability of SB 266 to draft comprehensive plans involves legal issues for which you may wish to seek the advice of counsel. MDP, however, does not believe that comprehensive plans drafted under pre-October 1, 2025, legal requirements need to be amended now, prior to adoption, to comply with the Principles. Rather, the department suggests the following approaches to incorporating the new Principles, depending on final plan adoption date. 

- If the Draft 2025 Somerset County Comprehensive Plan Amendment is adopted **before October 1, 2025** , MDP recommends that jurisdictions address the Visions in the adopted plan as they would have if the General Assembly had not adopted SB 266. Jurisdictions should consider, however, acknowledging the adoption of SB 266 and including a statement that the jurisdiction will work with MDP to implement the Principles as part of plan implementation. 

- If the Draft 2025 Somerset County Comprehensive Plan Amendment is adopted **after October 1, 2025,** MDP recommends that jurisdictions acknowledge and describe the Principles in the adopted plan and indicate that the jurisdiction will work with MDP to implement the Principles as part of plan implementation. Attached to this review is a model one-page Principles comprehensive plan insert that jurisdictions may use for the purpose of this acknowledgement.

###### **5. Affordable Housing Chapter – Analysis** 

MDP notes the Draft Plan’s Affordable Housing Chapter is well-developed and reflects recent changes in COMAR’s Land Use Article and Housing and Community Development Article with respect to affordable housing.  However, in referencing Maryland’s House Bill 90 (2010), the Draft Plan Affordable Housing Chapter and “Appendix C: Housing Needs Assessment Method” tangentially discusses the Land Use Article (LUA) section 3-114 requirement for jurisdictions to “include an assessment of fair housing to ensure that the local jurisdiction is affirmatively furthering fair housing”. 

LUA 3-114 does not define what an assessment of fair housing entails, but MDP developed <u>this webpage</u> , which describes available fair housing resources and best practices and how jurisdictions might consider using them in their housing elements. It includes a section on data and mapping resources which may help the city with a quantitative assessment. It also includes self-assessment and community engagement questions which the city might use (or expand upon) to conduct a qualitative assessment of fair housing. MDP recommends that the city includes more detail in the Affordable Housing Chapter about how it will complete a community or regional assessment of fair housing within the Draft Plan’s planning horizon. 

MDP’s research into affirmatively furthering fair housing (AFFH) has shown that a traditional US HUD assessment of fair housing includes an analysis of the following four housing issues in a community. 

1. **Patterns of segregation/integration:** Areas within the jurisdiction that are residentially segregated by protected class 

2. **Racially or Ethnically Concentrated Areas of Poverty (R/ECAPS):** US HUD defines R/ECAPS in metropolitan areas as census tracts with a non-white population of 50 percent or more and a poverty rate of 40 percent or more (or a poverty rate that is three or more times the average tract poverty rate for the metropolitan area, whichever threshold is lower). For rural areas, HUD lowers the non-white percentage threshold to 20 percent. 

3. **Disparities in access to opportunity:** Areas within the community/jurisdiction that provide access to opportunity, such as good schools, medical facilities, employment centers, positive public health outcomes, and low crime rates. A fair housing assessment would consider if protected classes have less access to such areas. 

4. **Disproportionate housing needs:** An analysis considering whether certain areas or populations within a community, particularly protected classes, have disproportionate housing needs than other areas or populations. 

The LUA 3-114 does not state that these four issues must be included in an assessment of fair housing in a housing element, nor is MDP setting any standards for what must be included. MDP encourages the city to discuss the legal requirements of the assessment with its attorney. MDP staff are also available to meet with the city and discuss fair housing needs and objectives in more detail.

##### **8.1** Habitats of Threatened and Endangered Species 

The primary Maryland law that governs the legal listing of threatened and endangered species is the Nongame and Endangered Species Conservation Act (Annotated Code of Maryland 10-2A-01), enacted in 1975. The Act is supported by regulations (Code of Maryland Regulations, COMAR 08.03.08) that define listing criteria for endangered, threatened, in need of conservation, and endangered extirpated species; lists the species included in each category; establishes the purpose and intent of research and collection permits; and lists prohibited activities. Maryland regulations may be found <u>online.</u> 

The <u>Maryland Natural Heritage Program</u> tracks the status of over 1,250 native plants and animals that are among the rarest in Maryland and most in need of conservation efforts as elements of our State's natural diversity. Of these species, the Maryland Department of Natural Resources officially recognizes 566 species and subspecies as endangered, threatened, in need of conservation, or endangered extirpated. Only 39, or 7% of the total tracked species, are listed by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service as nationally endangered or threatened. 

Federal and State laws have been put in place to protect the habitats of threatened and endangered species. Somerset County has primary regulatory authority over most development activity as it relates to potentially effected habitats. This means the County plays a key role in ensuring property owners comply with Federal and State laws. 

**Threatened and Endangered Species Definitions** 

**Threatened Species:** means any species of flora or fauna which appears likely, within the foreseeable future, to become endangered including any species determined to be a "threatened species" pursuant to the federal Endangered Species Act of 1973, 16 U.S.C. §§1531-1543. 

**Endangered Species** : means any species whose continued existence as a viable component of the State's flora or fauna is determined to be in jeopardy including any species determined to be an "endangered species" pursuant to the federal Endangered Species Act of 1973, 16 U.S.C. §§1531-1543.

###### **8.1.1** _Sensitive Species Project Review Areas_ 

The Maryland Department of Natural Resources (DNR) <u>Sensitive Species Project Review Areas (SSPRA)</u> is a digital map layer that represent the general locations of documented rare, threatened, and endangered species. The data layer incorporates several types of regulated areas under the Critical Area Criteria and other areas of concern statewide, including Natural Heritage Areas, Listed Species Sites, Other or Locally Significant Habitat Areas, Colonial Waterbird Sites, Nontidal Wetlands of Special State Concern, and Geographic Areas of Particular Concern. 

This data layer provides an overview of nearly all state-regulated and designated areas involving sensitive and listed species. However, it does not supersede, and should not be used instead of, the State's Nontidal Wetlands Guidance maps. These areas are depicted on Map 8-4. 

Sensitive Species Project Review Areas include lands with habitat of federal-listed species, lands with habitat of state-listed species, and lands of concern to DNR due to the habitat of rare species that are not currently on either list. Federal-listed species lands make up 29,104.4 acres, state-listed species lands make up 73,208.97 acres, and lands with other species of concern make up 3,115.21 acres.
