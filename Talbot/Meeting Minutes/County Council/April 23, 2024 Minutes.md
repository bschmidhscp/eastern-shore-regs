VI. <u>Update by Talbot Humane – Patty Crankshaw-Quimby, Executive Director, Talbot Humane – Ms.</u> Crankshaw-Quimby provided statistical information on the number and types of animals taken in at Talbot Humane and the number of animals that were adopted, reunited with their owners or transferred to other rescue groups.  Ms. Crankshaw-Quimby also provided statistical information on the number of animals that were spayed or neutered and microchipped, and the number of animals that were fostered. She stated that the intake of animals at the shelter increased by 18% from 2022 to 2023 and that nationally, animals are staying at shelters for longer periods of time, increasing the likelihood of disease and behavioral issues. She stated that one of the main goals of Talbot Humane is to intervene with these animals through programming prior to their entering the shelter system. Regarding the animal control function of Talbot Humane, Ms. Crankshaw-Quimby stated that officers made 380 animal control calls, 90% of which were for bite cases, stray animals and investigations of violations, and that 70% required follow-up visits.  She stated that in 2023, 1,300 animals (at public and private animal shelters in Talbot County) were spayed or neutered; 2024 is the 20<sup>th</sup> year of Talbot Humane’s spay/neuter program which has resulted in a 48% reduction in intake of animals since the program began in 2004.  Ms. Crankshaw-Quimby noted other services provided by Talbot Humane, including distribution of pet food and other pet supplies, and microchipping.  She stated that in 2024, Talbot Humane won a competitive Maryland Department of Agriculture grant in the sum of $56,000 to provide free spay/neuter services to qualified residents of Talbot and Dorchester counties.  Ms. Crankshaw-Quimby expressed her appreciation to the many volunteers who contribute countless hours toward caring for the animals and attending community events on behalf of Talbot Humane, noting the upcoming “Strutt Your Mutt Dog Walk” at Oxford Day on Saturday, April 27, 2024, and the Frederick Running Festival on Saturday, May 5, 2024.  Ms. Crankshaw-Quimby concluded her presentation by offering Council members an opportunity to tour the Talbot Humane facility.  Council members expressed their appreciation to Ms. Crankshaw-Quimby for her work with the animals.  Talbot Humane will continue to update the Council on a regular or as-needed basis. 

VII. <u>Presentation by Talbot County Agricultural Community – Shannon Dill, Extension Educator –</u> AgFS, University of Maryland Extension – Talbot County; Shawn Smith, District Manager, Talbot Soil Conservation District; John Swaine, III, Chairman, Board of Supervisors, Talbot Soil Conservation District and Vice President of Talbot County Farm Bureau; John Barga, County Executive Director, USDA Farm Service Agency – Ms. Dill stated that the mission of the University of Maryland Extension Office in Talbot County, and its partner agencies, is to support agricultural profitability and sustainability in the county by providing the community with unbiased research-based information related to agriculture by focusing on production, regulatory compliance, marketing and educational programs.  Ms. Dill briefed the Council about a new workforce development internship program which is funded by the United States Department of Agriculture National Institute on Food and Agriculture (USDA NIFA) to advance agriculture-related sciences and careers in hopes that the next generation will pursue agricultural careers upon entering the workforce, thus helping to ensure the long-term viability of agriculture.  Ms. Dill provided statistical information from the latest USDA Census which is completed every five years; the last USDA Census was completed in 2017.  She stated that there are currently 357 farms in Talbot County, an increase of 13% since the last survey was completed; however, the size of the average farm is down, to approximately 270 acres; production expenses have increased 45% since COVID19 and the marketing value has increased 60%, noting that Talbot County is 12<sup>th</sup> in the state with regard to the market value of products sold.   Ms. Dill concluded her presentation by stating that the flowering vegetation in fields is the cover crop, tillage radish, which when plowed under in the spring, helps to break up compacted soils; and that slow-moving farm equipment is once again on

the roadways, and she urged motorists to use caution and to have patience as the equipment travels from field to field throughout the county.  Mr. Smith stated that the Talbot Soil Conservation District, which recently celebrated its 66<sup>th</sup> year, provides technical assistance for conservation practices for agricultural operations, promotes educational conservation outreach, and ensures proper construction planning and delivery.  He stated that each year, the Soil Conservation District holds a conservation tour; in October 2023, the 2022 Cooperator of the Year Award was present to John Trax and Keith Shortall, whose families have farmed together for more than 60 years; they recently worked with the Soil Conservation District on a number of projects to address water quality and nutrient runoff, including completion of two grass waterways.  Mr. Smith outlined other accomplishments, including the planting of over 33,000 acres of cover crops by 74 farmers, stated that the District worked with more farmers than in the previous year to install more best management practices, and reviewed approximately 160 erosion and sediment control plans in the county.  Mr. Swaine stated that the Talbot County Farm Bureau, was established in 1956 to work with the Maryland Farm Bureau and the American Farm Bureau Federation to provide its members with a better understanding of agriculture.  He outlined several priorities of the Talbot County Farm Bureau, including support of all day Sunday Deer Hunting on private land, an Anti-Discrimination Statement and Permanent Funding for Nutrient Management. Mr. Barga stated that the USDA Farm Service Agency provides farmers with not only a strong safety net program, but also focuses on conservation, targeting conservation farm programs and farm loan programs by working with farmers on wetland restorations, wildlife preservation and soil and water erosion control; the Agency also provides programs to assist and support farmers following natural disasters as well as direct operating loans, particularly for beginning farmers and ranchers, and minority and women farmers and ranchers.  The Council members expressed their appreciation to the representatives of the agricultural community in attendance for helping to ensure that agriculture remains a vital part of the economy of Talbot County.  Representatives of the agricultural community will continue to update the Council on a regular or as-needed basis. 

## VIII. <u>Public Hearing:</u> 

Resolution No. 356, A RESOLUTION TO FORMALLY DECLINE TO ACCEPT THE OFFER OF DEDICATION FOR THE PAPER STREET PORTION OF _CALVERT AVENUE_ AS SHOWN ON A SUBDIVISION PLAT ENTITLED “ _MAP OF ‘RIO VISTA’ DEVELOPMENT_ ” DATED FEBRUARY 1954, RECORDED AMONG THE LAND RECORDS OF TALBOT COUNTY, MARYLAND AT PLAT BOOK J.T.B. 8, FOLIO 11, AND TO AUTHORIZE THE EXECUTION OF A QUITCLAIM DEED TO CONVEY A 4,195 SQUARE FOOT PORTION OF THE PAPER STREET ADJACENT TO 226 TYLER AVENUE, ST. MICHAELS, MARYLAND TO THE OWNERS OF SUCH PROPERTY, DR. GRAYSON R. BOWERS AND TERESA M. BOWERS, FOR NO MONETARY CONSIDERATION, was read into the record and brought forward for public hearing.  Prior to the public hearing, County Attorney, Patrick Thomas, stated that the plat referenced in the title of the resolution depicts several streets and roadways in the Rio Vista development that were offered to be dedicated to public use, including several “paper streets” that were never opened by the County and one of which is a portion of Calvert Avenue which runs between Forest Avenue and Monroe Street.  Mr. Thomas stated that the rear of Dr. and Mrs. Bower’s property abuts that paper street portion of Calvert Avenue which is the subject of Resolution No. 356.  Members of the public were provided an opportunity to comment on the legislation.  The record on Resolution No. 356 will remain open.  Resolution No. 356 is eligible for vote on Tuesday, May 14, 2024.

Bill No. 1552, A BILL TO AMEND CHAPTER 11 OF THE TALBOT COUNTY CODE (ALCOHOLIC BEVERAGES) FOR THE PURPOSE OF GENERALLY EXEMPTING THOMPSON PARK, LOCATED IN THE TOWN OF EASTON, FROM THE 500-FOOT PUBLIC PARK SETBACK REQUIREMENT FOR NEW OFF-SALE LICENSES, was read into the record by the Clerk and brought forward for vote.  Prior to the vote, County Attorney, Patrick Thomas, stated that the legislation was requested to be introduced by Bluepoint Hospitality Group (Easton) since under the County’s current alcoholic beverages code, new off-sale license establishments must be located at least 500 feet from schools, correctional facilities, and parks, with the exception of Thompson Park in Easton, but only for Class I (Alcohol dispensary Bistros), which includes The Wardroom owned by Bluepoint Hospitality Group.  Mr. Thomas stated that, if the legislation is approved, it would delete the reference to Class I (Alcohol Dispensary Bistros), thereby allowing any off-sale establishment within 500 feet of Thompson Park.  The Council voted to approved Bill No. 1552 by voting 5 – 0 as follows:
