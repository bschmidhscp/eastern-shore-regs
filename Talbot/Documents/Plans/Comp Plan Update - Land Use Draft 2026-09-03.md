4. Land Use - 1
Introduction
Talbot County’s rural landscape has evolved from its rich history as a farming and maritime community. The
landscape is dotted with classic towns and villages that developed at crossroads, landings and former mill sites
to serve seafood, boat building, and agriculture industries.
This plan seeks to maintain Talbot County's rural pattern of settlement by guiding urban development to
Population Centers where public infrastructure exists, and by discouraging development in the County’s most
rural and environmentally sensitive areas.
Role of the Land Use Element
As framework for organizing land use policy, the County has been divided into three primary land use policy
sectors: Development and Growth, Rural Reserve, and Sensitive Areas. For each sector, guiding principles are
outlined and ensuing implementation measures are coordinated for their consistency with the principles.
Community character, a pattern scale, and a mix of land uses can be readily discerned within the sectors.
The Plan’s land use strategies focus on limiting development in environmentally sensitive and rural areas
while encouraging growth in the incorporated towns and their adjacent Designated Growth areas. These
strategies are designed to accomplish the County’s land use visions and goals.
In addition, some village communities have been identified as Priority Funding Areas (PFAs), where the state
may make economic infrastructure investments to support sustainable development. Future development in
PFAs should be compatible with the pattern, scale and mix of land uses proposed in this Comprehensive
Plan.
This chapter presents the existing zoning districts, existing land uses within the County, and future land uses
as well as goals and objectives to maintain future growth.
Goals and Objectives
Talbot County aims to provide balanced land and water resource usage to ensure efficient and sustainable
community development. To fulfill this, the following goals and objectives have been identified:
1.
Promote and maintain a well-planned pattern of land and water resource use, through which
compatible and efficient development is concentrated in areas where environmental impacts will be
minimized.
2.
Retain the County’s rural character.
3.
Ensure that anticipated growth occurs in appropriate locations with suitable public and private
community services.
Relationship with Local and Regional Policies
The 2016 Comprehensive Plan outlined regional and local policies that the County should accomplish. Those
policies are still recognized and are outlined in Table 4-1, along with all of the policies for land uses,
development and growth, Rural Reserve Planning Areas, and sensitive areas.

Existing Zoning Framework
Zoning defers from land use because it establishes dimensional standards and requirements that apply within
that zoning district based upon the current allowed uses of the land. Article II within the County’s Zoning
Ordinance Code establishes the zoning districts as well as all the regulations within each. Each zoning district
is described below, and further details can be found in the County’s Code.
Agricultural Conservation (AC): The AC district is primarily rural agricultural and low-density residential uses.
This district encourages agricultural activities, forests, agribusiness uses and limited single-family residential
development.
Countryside Preservation (CP): The CP District is characterized by rural agricultural and low-density residential
uses. It protects farmland, forests, and open spaces, prevents sprawl, and provides physical and visual
boundaries to growth areas. Land within this district should be permanently protected by agricultural and
conservation easements.
General Commercial (GC): This district has moderate-intensity commercial uses. It provides opportunities for
a broad range of commercial activities including retail, wholesale, storage, and contracting activities.
Limited Commercial (LC): This district has low-intensity commercial uses. It provides opportunities for
commercial and office development.
Limited Industrial (LI): This district is characterized by low-intensity manufacturing uses. It provides
opportunities for light industrial/office-research uses that provide employment and services.
Rural Conservation (RC): This district is characterized by natural environments (wetlands, forests, or
abandoned fields) and resource development activities (agriculture, forestry, fisheries, or aquaculture). It
conserves the irreplaceable agricultural, forested, and natural environmental character of the County.
Residential Rural (RR): This district is low-intensity residential use. It protects the environment and preserves
natural resources in the rural areas of the County while allowing a limited degree of residential development.
Development is in the form of large or clustered lots outside of protection areas.
Town Conservation (TC): This district is primarily agricultural and low-density residential uses. It prevents
sprawl, preserves the character and identity of towns, and preserves the opportunity for orderly, well-planned,
future growth of these areas through re-subdivision and re-development after annexation into the towns.
Town Residential (TR): This district is characterized by existing moderate-intensity residential uses. It
identifies existing residential neighborhoods near incorporated towns and allows compatible infill
development while preserving existing natural habitat.
Village Hamlet (VH): This district has low or moderate-intensity residential and limited commercial uses. It’s
intended for residential use with limited appropriate commercial use that serves the residents of the village

Development and Growth
Designated Growth and Future Growth
In Talbot County, Growth Areas are those that are meant to accommodate commercial, institutional, future
residential, and mixed-use development. Key Municipalities in the County’s identified growth areas include
Easton, St. Michaels, Oxford, and Trappe. Limited growth is anticipated in Queen Anne, so it is not
considered in the Development and Growth sector. Additionally, there are 22 villages within the County, but
these areas cannot accommodate stabilized growth.
The four municipalities discussed above have their own planning and zoning authority along with an existing
infrastructure network capable of supporting planned growth. Each municipality typically develops their own
Comprehensive Plan that will identify any potential annexation areas, conservation, redevelopment, infill
development, and/or future expansion. The County will continue to work closely with these Municipalities
and Villages to ensure a cohesive vision for the future. Doing so will further this Plan’s objectives to direct
growth to the County’s existing population centers and help deflect development pressure from agricultural
and environmentally sensitive areas. Development is anticipated to occur in these areas only after annexation
and will then be regulated by the municipalities.
Existing Commercial
Certain small, isolated areas of the County are zoned General Commercial (GC), Limited Commercial (LC),
or Limited Industry (LI), based on past land uses such as country stores, vegetable canneries, etc. No
significant growth or expansion is anticipated for these sites.
Community Character
Talbot’s County character includes villages in the residential areas, which are low or moderate density historic
residential communities with limited neighborhood commercial and other uses or services. They are an
important component of rural character, providing a pleasingly scaled and textured contrast to the County’s
more urban areas.
Villages are recognized for their significant heritage and pattern of development. Villages are designated
Community Character in order to safeguard these attributes while providing for some measure of growth and
redevelopment. In its Smart Growth Act of 1998, the State of Maryland defines a rural village as an:
“...unincorporated area that is primarily residential, including an area with historic qualities, that is located in an
otherwise rural or agricultural area and for which new growth, if any, would derive primarily from in-fill development or
limited peripheral expansion.”
As discussed in the 2016 Comprehensive Plan, villages should be required to maintain their sense of place as
identified by their existing architectural character, scale, mix of uses and density of development. Infill shall
be limited to completing development on existing lots of record at the density permitted. All village areas
should be re-examined as part of a comprehensive rezoning based on this Plan.

Rural Residential
Other moderate density residential communities are identified in this plan as Rural Residential and are also
designated as Limited Development Areas (LDA) in the County’s Chesapeake Bay Critical Area Plan. Though
these areas are largely developed, some infill and redevelopment is anticipated.  During the second half of the
twentieth century, Talbot County experienced suburban style large lot growth, characterized by uniform
subdivisions that consumed farm and forest with curvilinear and cul-de-sac street patterns. Largely built out,
these developments have little if any additional growth capacity. The County does not foresee permitting
more development of this type. Rather, the primary emphasis in these areas is to ensure that infill and
redevelopment is done in an environmentally sensitive manner.
Rural Reserve
The County’s rural reserve contains the majority of the area’s agriculture (labeled Agriculture and zoned
Agricultural Conservation, AC) and the boundary area between urban areas and the County’s rural lands
(labeled and zoned Countryside Preservation, CP). The Rural Reserve is designated to maintain a critical mass
of cropland, pasture, and forest to support agriculture and define the edge between urban and rural Talbot
County. The two sub-units of the Reserve are discussed below.
Agriculture
The agriculture planning area encompasses the bulk of rural land in the eastern half of the County. These
areas are characterized by farms, forestry, open space, low-density single-family homes, and agriculturally
related commercial and industrial establishments.
Agricultural and forestry activities are the preferred land uses within these areas. Future residential
development in the agriculture planning area should be designed to preserve productive agricultural lands,
woodlands, open space, environmentally sensitive resources and rural character. Conservation of agricultural
lands and open space through purchase of development rights (PDR) programs is encouraged in this region,
which is identified as a Priority Preservation Area for agriculture (see Chapter 8, Natural Resources
Conservation). Certain County farm and forest lands have been permanently protected through the
Maryland Agricultural Land Preservation Program (MALPF); a purchase of development rights program
established by the state in 1977. The County should continue its participation in this program to meet its
agricultural land preservation goal.
These lands are generally classified Tier IV in the Maryland regulations for subdivision on septic systems
known as SB 236 (see Chapter 9 – Water Resources for more information). Clustering is required for the
majority of lots in new residential subdivisions permitted in this planning area. Clustering shall be designed to
maintain agricultural use on the remaining nonresidential portion of the parcel. Permanent land preservation,
mandatory clustering and lower residential densities have successfully conserved valuable agricultural land and
open space, while allowing landowners to retain a degree of development potential and equity. This approach
should be continued.
Countryside Preservation
Countryside Preservation, zoned as CP, is an important sector of the Rural Reserve to encourage the
preservation of rural agriculture and rural low-density residential uses. Land uses within this area are intended
to conserve and protect the agricultural lands and open space.

Sensitive Areas
Talbot County’s sensitive lands are composed of the tidal perimeter of the County along the Chesapeake Bay
and its local tributaries. The non-tidal portions in the western half also require special attention since all lands
here have a sensitive relationship with the natural environment. The Sensitive Areas sector was broken down
into the Chesapeake Bay Critical Area and Western Rural Conservation.
Chesapeake Bay Critical Area
The County adopted a Chesapeake Bay Critical Area Plan which affects all waterfront areas of the County
1,000 feet landward from the shoreline or the inland edge of tidal wetlands. In the Critical Area, Talbot
County zoning districts are concurrent with its Critical Area designation. The Rural Conservation (RC) zoning
district corresponds to the Resource Conservation Area (RCA) designation in the Critical Area Program.
These areas are characterized by natural environments, agriculture, forestry and fisheries and it is the County’s
intent to retain these areas in such uses. Only low-density residential development is permitted, provided such
development is located outside of habitat protection areas and can demonstrate that water quality and habitat
have been maintained or improved. Within the Critical Area, detailed regulations have been adopted which
direct, manage and control residential, commercial and industrial development to minimize adverse impacts
of growth. Critical Areas are discussed in greater detail in Chapter 8, Natural Resource Conservation.
Regulations are detailed in the County Zoning Ordinance.
Critical Area law makes some provision for growth allocation, or re-designating land for more intense
development. The total land area available for re-designation is limited to five percent of the County’s
Resource Conservation Area (RCA) lands. Growth allocation should only be permitted where it advances the
land use policies and objectives of this Comprehensive Plan.
Western Rural Conservation
The upland portions that are not a critical area of Talbot County’s western necks are zoned Western Rural
Conservation (WRC) in recognition of the high concentration of sensitive natural areas in close proximity to
tributaries of the Chesapeake Bay. This planning area features a mix of agriculture, low-density residential,
and natural resource areas.
These narrow land areas have few routes to the inland parts of the County. Flooding, traffic, and other road
obstructions have demonstrated legitimate cause for concern, should development overcome the capacity for
safe transit through these areas. Conserving the agriculture, forestry, recreational and resource conservation
uses that form the character of these areas is a high priority. The WRC zone will be targeted for development
controls and resource enhancement programs designed to protect natural resources while providing for
limited, low-density residential development with appropriate safeguards for resource protection.
Greenway
Greenways are hubs of natural land linked together by a network of corridors. Greenways provide scenic
qualities and include places or trails with historic and cultural values providing educational, scenic,
recreational or economic benefits to the community. Greenway hubs are significant areas that provide for
wildlife habitat and biodiversity. Streams and forests are prime ecological greenways. Talbot County updated
their 2004 Green Infrastructure Plan in 2021 and named it Cleaner, Greener Talbot: A Plan for Future Livability.
The new plan provides the County with a modern methodology for prioritizing its land according to resource

Future Land Use Framework
Relationship Between Future Land Use and Zoning
Ensuring that both future land use and zoning are working in tandem to shape the future of Talbot County is 
important. Future Land Use is the broad plan a community creates for how its land should develop over time.
Annexation and development within the municipalities identified growth areas should occur incrementally, 
with properties on the inner ring receiving the higher priority for annexation and development. “Leapfrog” 
development of properties at low densities on the outer perimeter of growth areas shall be avoided, along 
with “pipestem” or “panhandle” annexations by towns. The County supports orderly town expansion and 
discourages premature development at the growth areas’ extremities. Future commercial development is also 
encouraged to locate within the towns or as infill and redevelopment of existing commercial areas. Large-
scale commercial uses, including shopping centers and ‘big box’ retail will not be permitted in the identified 
growth areas while under County land use authority, but may be developed upon annexation into a town.
Business and industrial uses will also be encouraged to locate within the towns but may be located in 
appropriate County zoning districts. Commercial and industrial areas within growth areas contain parcels 
zoned for such activities. The purpose of these sites is to provide appropriate and adequate areas for future 
employment. They are not appropriate locations for large-scale retail. There are a limited number of un-
annexed acres in the County designated for industrial and commercial development, primarily surrounding 
the Town of Easton.
Anticipated Future Land Use
The County does not anticipate any future land use changes or growth within the County for this 
Comprehensive Plan update. As discussed, the identified four municipalities have their own planning and 
zoning authority along with an existing infrastructure network capable of supporting planned growth. Each 
municipality developed their own Comprehensive Plan that identifies potential annexation areas, 
conservation, redevelopment, infill development, and/or future expansion. Refer to each municipality’s 
current Comprehensive Plan for any future land uses or growth within the County.
